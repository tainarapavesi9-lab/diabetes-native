I Counted Carbs for 600 Kids a Day for 31 Years. At 61, I Couldn't Figure Out My Own Blood Sugar.

I ate the "right" breakfast, took my walks and still watched my morning numbers creep up. What finally made sense wasn't another diet. It was understanding why my cells had stopped listening to insulin.

A reader story, as told to the Grayfield Review ·
Names and some details have been changed for privacy.

For 31 years I ran a school cafeteria in Ohio. I planned meals for about 600 kids a day, and I could tell you the carbs in a dinner roll without looking it up.

So when my doctor told me at 59 that my blood sugar was "creeping into the prediabetes range," I was almost offended. Me? I knew nutrition better than anyone I knew.

I did everything he said. I cut the white bread. I swapped soda for water. I walked 30 minutes after dinner with my husband, Ray.

And for two years, my morning numbers kept climbing anyway.

"I felt like I was failing a test I had been teaching for three decades."

The afternoon crash nobody warned me about

The numbers were only half of it. The part that really wore me down was how I felt.

About an hour after lunch, a wave of tiredness would roll in so hard I'd sit down "for a minute" and wake up at 3:30. Then came the cravings. Not a polite craving. A drive-to-the-store-for-cookies craving.

My mother lived with type 2 diabetes for 20 years. I remember her pricking her finger at the kitchen table, and I remember the day they talked about her feet. That memory scared me more than any number on a meter.

The "healthy" breakfast that was working against me

Here is the first thing that surprised me. My breakfast was the problem, and it looked perfect on paper.

A glass of orange juice. A banana. Whole wheat toast with a little jam. No sugar in my coffee. I was proud of that breakfast.

But when I finally wore a glucose monitor for two weeks, the graph after that meal looked like a mountain. Juice and ripe fruit on an empty stomach send sugar into the blood fast, and my body was struggling to clear it.

Worth knowingFruit juice is absorbed much faster than whole fruit because the fiber is removed. Pairing carbohydrates with protein, fat and fiber can slow how quickly sugar reaches the bloodstream.

Changing breakfast helped a little. But it didn't explain why my body had become so bad at handling sugar in the first place. That was the question nobody had answered for me.

Why your body stops "listening" to insulin after 50

The answer came from my daughter, Megan, who is a pharmacist. She drew it on a napkin at Sunday dinner, and it was the first time it clicked.

"Mom, insulin is a key," she said. "Every cell in your body has a lock. When you eat, insulin opens the lock so sugar can go inside and become energy."

"But with age, belly weight, stress and bad sleep, the locks get sticky. The key still turns up, but the door won't open. Doctors call it insulin resistance."

When the locks don't open, sugar stays in your blood. Your body pumps out even more insulin to force the door. That's the roller coaster: a spike after meals, a crash, and then the cravings.

I sat there with that napkin and felt something I hadn't felt in two years. Not guilt. Relief. I wasn't lazy. I wasn't failing. My cells had simply gotten worse at using the key.

Does any of this sound like you?

Megan gave me a short list of signs that often show up when the body is struggling with sugar. I checked every single one.

  • You feel a heavy energy crash one or two hours after meals
  • You crave sweets or bread in the afternoon, almost like clockwork
  • You carry extra weight around your middle that won't move, even when you eat less
  • Your morning readings keep creeping up even though you "eat right"

If two or more of these sound familiar, talk to your doctor about checking for insulin resistance. And keep reading, because what I found next is what finally gave me back my afternoons.

What I tried before (and why it wasn't enough)

I want to be honest about this part, because I spent a lot of money figuring it out.

  1. Cutting carbs hard. My numbers dipped, but I was miserable and irritable, and I quit in three weeks.
  2. Cinnamon capsules from the drugstore. I took them for four months. I honestly couldn't tell any difference.
  3. Apple cider vinegar before meals. It burned my throat, and I couldn't keep it up.
  4. A "blood sugar tea" from social media. Expensive, and the ingredient list was mostly flavoring.

Every one of these went after one small piece of the problem. None of them went after the part Megan had drawn on the napkin: how my cells were using the key.
